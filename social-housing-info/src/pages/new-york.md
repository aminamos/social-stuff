# New York Rent Stabilization

New York City has the country's oldest and largest rent regulation system. Roughly **one million apartments** — close to half the city's rental stock — are rent-stabilized. If you want to understand how modern rent stabilization works in practice, New York is the canonical case.

## A short history

- **1943–1950**: Federal wartime price controls include rent. When they expire, New York State enacts its own emergency rent control.
- **1969**: The Rent Stabilization Law creates the modern two-tier system: rent-controlled units (built before 1947, with continuous occupancy) and rent-stabilized units (buildings of six or more units built between 1947 and 1973).
- **1997**: Major rollback. Vacancy decontrol allows landlords to deregulate apartments once they hit a rent threshold ($2,000/month) and the tenant vacates. Luxury decontrol deregulates units where the tenant earns over $175,000.
- **2019** — Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act: The biggest tenant win in decades. Eliminates vacancy and luxury decontrol, closes the "preferential rent" loophole, limits rent increases from major capital improvements, and extends protections statewide.

## How the Rent Guidelines Board works

Every year, the **Rent Guidelines Board** (RGB) — a nine-member panel appointed by the mayor — sets the maximum percentage increase for the roughly two million tenants in stabilized apartments. The board hears testimony from landlords, tenants, and staff economists, then votes on one- and two-year lease increases.

## What Mamdani's rent freeze would do

Zohran Mamdani's proposal is straightforward: set the RGB's annual increase to **0%** across the board. For tenants in stabilized units, their rent would not rise year-over-year. The policy would not deregulate any units, change the building stock, or alter the existing stabilization framework — it's a change to the *rate*, not the *structure*.

## Key facts

- **Coverage**: ~1 million apartments in NYC are rent-stabilized. Another ~16,000 are under the older rent-control system.
- **Who lives there**: Stabilized tenants are disproportionately low-income, elderly, and people of color. Median household income in stabilized units is about $47,000 — well below the city median.
- **Vacancy rate**: NYC's overall rental vacancy rate hovers around 1.4%, among the lowest in the country. The rate for stabilized units is even lower.
