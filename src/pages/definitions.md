# Definitions

Most people use "rent control" as a catch-all, but in housing policy it refers to a family of laws that limit how much landlords can charge and how fast rent can rise. Understanding the distinctions matters — the evidence on each approach is very different.

<ul class="def-list">
<li>
  <span class="def-term">Rent Control (First Generation)</span>
  A hard price ceiling that caps rent at a fixed amount, often tied to the unit itself regardless of who lives there. These laws were enacted in many cities after World War II. In their strictest form — sometimes called a "rent freeze" — the nominal rent cannot rise at all. Most economists agree that strict, long-running first-generation rent control reduces housing supply and quality over time, as landlords exit the market or defer maintenance.
</li>

<li>
  <span class="def-term">Rent Stabilization (Second Generation)</span>
  A more flexible system where rents can rise, but only by a percentage set each year by a regulatory board. The allowed increase is typically tied to an index of operating costs. Tenants have a right to lease renewal, and evictions require cause. This is the system New York City has used since 1969. Most modern rent regulation in the United States falls into this category.
</li>

<li>
  <span class="def-term">Rent Freeze</span>
  A temporary or permanent halt to all rent increases. During the COVID-19 pandemic, many jurisdictions enacted emergency rent freezes. Zaran Mamdani's proposal would set the annual increase at 0% for stabilized units — meaning tenants in those units would see no rent hikes.
</li>

<li>
  <span class="def-term">Vacancy Control</span>
  A provision that keeps a unit regulated even after a tenant moves out — the rent ceiling stays with the unit rather than resetting to market rate. Vacancy control is one of the most debated features: it protects affordability long-term but is also where most supply-side concerns are concentrated. New York weakened its vacancy control in 1997 and largely eliminated it in 2019.
</li>

<li>
  <span class="def-term">Just-Cause Eviction</span>
  A law requiring landlords to cite a specific, legally valid reason to evict a tenant — nonpayment, lease violation, nuisance, or the owner moving in. This prevents landlords from circumventing rent regulation by simply evicting tenants and re-listing the unit at market rate. Often paired with rent stabilization.
</li>
</ul>

<div class="callout">
<h3>Why the terminology matters</h3>

<p>When you hear "rent control doesn't work," the speaker is almost always referring to <strong>first-generation</strong> rent control — hard caps from the 1940s–1970s. The modern policies on the table today are <strong>rent stabilization</strong> systems, and the empirical evidence on those is much more mixed. Conflating the two is the most common rhetorical move in this debate.</p>
</div>
