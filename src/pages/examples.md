# Global Examples

The claim that rent regulation *inevitably* reduces housing supply doesn't hold up against the evidence. Here are real-world cases where rent controls coexisted with — and in some cases accompanied — strong housing construction.

## Germany: Rent controls + high construction

Germany has one of the most regulated rental markets in the developed world, and it also builds more housing per capita than the United States. The key policy is the **Mietpreisbremse** (rent brake), introduced in 2015:

- Rents on new leases cannot exceed 10% above the local reference rent (the *Mietspiegel*).
- The *Mietspiegel* is calculated from actual rents in the area — it's a data-driven benchmark, not an arbitrary cap.
- Existing tenants are protected: rent increases are capped at 20% over three years (15% in tight markets).

Germany builds roughly **3.5 housing units per 1,000 people** annually, compared to about **2.5** in the US. The rent brake has not cratered construction — new builds are exempt for the first lease, which preserves the incentive to develop.

<h3>Key insight</h3>

<p>Germany's system works because it distinguishes between <strong>existing tenants</strong> (protected from displacement) and <strong>new construction</strong> (exempt from price caps on first lease). This preserves the incentive to build while keeping existing renters housed.</p>

## Minnesota: The 7% rule

Research from the University of Minnesota's Center for Urban and Regional Affairs, led by Professor **Ed Goetz**, has found that rent stabilization with annual increases capped at **7% or below** does not have a statistically significant negative effect on housing supply. The mechanism:

- At 7%, landlords can still recoup operating cost increases and earn a reasonable return.
- Developers factor the cap into their pro formas; it doesn't kill deals.
- Above 7%, the evidence of supply effects starts to emerge — but most modern stabilization regimes are well under that threshold.

The Twin Cities (Minneapolis and St. Paul) both enacted rent stabilization in 2021. Minneapolis caps increases at 3%, St. Paul at 3% with some exemptions. Both cities continue to see active construction.

## California: Statewide rent cap (2019)

California's **Tenant Protection Act** (AB 1482) caps annual rent increases at **5% plus local inflation** (CPI), up to a maximum of 10%. It also requires just-cause eviction for tenants who've lived in a unit for over a year.

Since passage, California has continued to build — and several cities have enacted *stricter* local caps that go below the state ceiling. The policy has not halted development in any major California market.

## Spain: National rent control (2023)

Spain enacted a national rent cap in 2023 — 2% for 2023, then 3% for 2024, with a new index-based system going forward. While it's too early for long-term supply data, early evidence does not show a construction pullback. Crucially, Spain paired the cap with tax incentives for landlords who rent at below-market rates, creating a carrot alongside the stick.

## What these cases share

Successful rent stabilization regimes tend to have these features:

1. **New construction is exempt** for some period, preserving developer incentives.
2. **The cap is tied to a real index** — inflation, operating costs, or local market conditions — rather than being an arbitrary number.
3. **They're paired with supply-side policies** — zoning reform, social housing investment, or tax incentives.
4. **The annual increase is modest but non-zero** — typically 3–7%, which covers cost growth without displacing tenants.

## The counter-examples (and why they don't apply)

Critics often cite **San Francisco's 1979 rent control** expansion, which a 2019 study (Diamond, McQuade, and Qian) found reduced rental supply by 15% and increased displacement. But that study examined a first-generation hard-cap system that applied to *all* units, including new construction — a design no modern proposal replicates.

Similarly, **Stockholm's** queue-based system (where rent is pegged to a negotiated "use value") has produced decade-long wait times, but that's a function of a fixed supply of public housing units, not the rent-setting mechanism itself.
